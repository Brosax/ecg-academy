- Recording chain — Filter bandwidth: Diagnostic ECG: low-frequency cut-off 0.05 Hz, or up to 0.67 Hz only with zero-phase-distortion (linear-phase/bidirectional) digital filters. High-frequency cut-off at least 150 Hz in adults and at least 250 Hz in children. 'Monitor' or 'muscle' settings (about 0.5-40 Hz) distort ST segments (false ST elevation or depression), shrink QRS amplitude and can hide pacemaker spikes and notches. () — Kligfield 2007 AHA/ACCF/HRS Part I (Circulation 2007;115:1306)
- Recording chain — Sampling rate: At least 500 samples/s for diagnostic 12-lead ECGs. Pacemaker-spike detection needs much higher internal sampling or dedicated spike detection, and machine-added pacing markers can be artefacts. () — Kligfield 2007 AHA/ACCF/HRS Part I
- Mains (AC) interference — Recognition: Regular fine oscillation at 50 Hz (Spain, Europe, China) or 60 Hz (USA, parts of Latin America) that thickens the baseline. Causes: poor skin contact, a broken lead wire, nearby equipment. Fix the electrode contact rather than relying on the notch filter alone. () — Kligfield 2007 AHA/ACCF/HRS Part I; IEC 60601-2-25:2011
- Technical artefacts — Artefact mimicking arrhythmia: Tremor (e.g. Parkinsonian, 4-6 Hz), shivering or tapping can mimic atrial flutter or fibrillation and even VT. Clues: the real QRS complexes keep 'marching through' at the underlying R-R interval; the artefact is missing or minimal in at least one lead; the patient's condition does not change. Misreading has led to unnecessary procedures. (all, especially limb leads) — Knight BP et al. Clinical consequences of electrocardiographic artifact mimicking ventricular tachycardia. N Engl J Med 1999;341:1270-1274; Baranchuk A et al. Electrocardiography pitfalls and artifacts: the 10 commandments. Crit Care Nurse 2009;29:67-73
- Technical artefacts — Finding the faulty electrode: A limb-electrode artefact appears in the two bipolar leads that use that electrode and is nearly absent from the third. RA artefact: I and II, not III. LA artefact: I and III, not II. LL artefact: II and III, not I. It is largest in the augmented lead named after that electrode. (I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF) — Aslanger E, Yalin K. Electromechanical association: a subtle electrocardiogram artifact. J Electrocardiol 2012;45:15-17; Kligfield 2007 AHA/ACCF/HRS Part I
- Baseline wander — Recognition and effect: Slow drift with respiration or movement. It can mimic or hide ST deviation. Correct it with skin preparation, a relaxed supine patient and proper (zero-phase) high-pass filtering, not by raising the cut-off in non-linear-phase filters. () — Kligfield 2007 AHA/ACCF/HRS Part I
- Other limb cable reversals — Signatures: LA/LL: I and II swapped, aVL and aVF swapped, III inverted, aVR unchanged. Subtle clue: the P wave is larger in I than in II, or III shows a terminally positive P. RA/LL: I, II and III inverted (II inverted, I and III swap with inversion), aVR and aVF swapped, aVL unchanged; looks like inferior Q waves or an ectopic atrial rhythm. RA/RL: lead II almost a flat line. LA/RL: lead III almost a flat line. Bilateral arm-leg reversals cause other near-flat or bizarre patterns. Swaps among RA/LA/LL leave V1-V6 unaffected; reversals involving RL change Wilson's terminal and alter V1-V6 slightly (Batchvarov 2007). (I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF) — Batchvarov VN, Malik M, Camm AJ. Europace 2007;9:1081-1090; Harrigan RA, Chan TC, Brady WJ. Electrocardiographic electrode misplacement, misconnection, and artifact. J Emerg Med 2012;43:1038-1044
- Precordial misplacement / transposition — Signatures: Swapped precordial cables break the smooth R-wave progression and T-wave evolution (for example, an isolated 'jump' in one lead). V1-V2 placed too high gives rSr', negative P in V1-V2, pseudo-septal Q waves and a Brugada-like ST. V1-V2 placed too low can hide a true pattern. Before serial comparison, check that electrode placement is identical. (V1-V6) — Batchvarov 2007 Europace; SCST Clinical Guidelines: Recording a standard 12-lead ECG (2017/2020); Kligfield 2007 AHA/ACCF/HRS Part I
- High right precordial leads — Deliberate use for Brugada: Recording V1-V2 in the 2nd and 3rd ICS increases sensitivity for the type 1 Brugada pattern. The diagnosis needs coved ST elevation >=2 mm in >=1 right precordial lead in the 2nd, 3rd or 4th ICS. Students must be able to tell deliberate high placement apart from accidental misplacement. (V1, V2) — Zeppenfeld K et al. 2022 ESC Guidelines for ventricular arrhythmias and prevention of SCD. Eur Heart J 2022;43:3997-4126; Antzelevitch C et al. J-Wave syndromes expert consensus conference report. Heart Rhythm 2016;13:e295
- Electrode placement in women — Breast tissue: Find landmarks by palpation and place V4-V6 on the chest wall, under the breast, at the standard horizontal level, rather than on top of breast tissue. Document any deviation from standard placement. (V3-V6) — SCST Clinical Guidelines by Consensus: Recording a standard 12-lead ECG (2017, rev. 2020); Kligfield 2007 AHA/ACCF/HRS Part I
- Hexaxial reference system — Lead angles: I 0 deg, II +60 deg, III +120 deg, aVF +90 deg, aVL -30 deg, aVR -150 deg (or +210 deg). The Cabrera (orderly) display aVL, I, -aVR, II, aVF, III makes the frontal-plane sequence continuous and is an AHA-endorsed option. (I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF) — Kligfield 2007 AHA/ACCF/HRS Part I
- Heart-rate measurement — Methods: Regular rhythm: 300 / number of large squares, or 1500 / number of small squares, between consecutive R waves (at 25 mm/s). Irregular rhythm: count the QRS complexes in the 10 s strip and multiply by 6. Paper speed and gain must be checked first. () — Kligfield 2007 AHA/ACCF/HRS Part I; Mason JW et al. AHA/ACCF/HRS Part II: Electrocardiography diagnostic statement list. Circulation 2007;115:1325
- Digital interval measurement — Global intervals: On digital ECGs, global PR, QRS and QT are measured from the earliest onset to the latest offset across the simultaneously recorded leads. This makes them systematically longer than single-lead manual measurements, which explains differences between machine and manual values. () — Kligfield 2007 AHA/ACCF/HRS Part I; Rautaharju PM et al. AHA/ACCF/HRS Part IV. Circulation 2009;119:e241
- Computer interpretation — Overreading: The automated statement must always be confirmed by a qualified reader. Its errors cluster in rhythm diagnosis (e.g. AF vs artefact, pacemaker rhythms), and it does not recognize electrode reversals reliably. () — Schläpfer J, Wellens HJ. Computer-interpreted electrocardiograms: benefits and limitations. J Am Coll Cardiol 2017;70:1183-1192; Kligfield 2007 AHA/ACCF/HRS Part I
- Patient preparation and position — Recording conditions: Patient supine and relaxed, with skin preparation (clean, dry, shaved if needed). Limb electrodes go on the limbs, not the torso, for a resting diagnostic ECG. Any non-standard condition (sitting, torso limb electrodes, extra leads, modified gain or speed) is written on the tracing, because posture and electrode position change axis, voltages and T waves. () — Kligfield 2007 AHA/ACCF/HRS Part I; SCST Clinical Guidelines: Recording a standard 12-lead ECG (2017/2020)

- Recording standard — paper speed, gain, grid, bandwidth: Paper speed is 25 mm/s and gain is 10 mm/mV, so a small square is 0.04 s and 0.1 mV, and a large square is 0.20 s and 0.5 mV. Check the calibration pulse before reading; half-standard gain makes voltages look smaller. Bandwidth: high-pass 0.05 Hz (or 0.67 Hz with a zero-phase filter); low-pass >=150 Hz in adults and 250 Hz in children. Recording is 10 s. () — AHA/ACCF/HRS 2007 Part I: The ECG and its technology (Kligfield P et al., Circulation 2007;115:1306-1324)
- Heart rate — small-square method: Rate = 1500 / number of small squares between R waves at 25 mm/s. For irregular rhythms, count QRS complexes on the 10-s ECG and multiply by 6. () — AHA/ACCF/HRS 2007 Part I (Kligfield P et al.); Goldberger's Clinical Electrocardiography, 10th ed., 2023
- Interval measurement — global measurement and computer over-reading: Measure PR, QRS and QT from the earliest onset in any lead to the latest offset in any lead on simultaneously recorded leads. Computer-measured intervals must be over-read by a physician. () — AHA/ACCF/HRS 2007 Part I (Kligfield P et al., Circulation 2007;115:1306-1324)
- Sinus node rhythms — bradycardia, pause, tachycardia, inappropriate sinus tachycardia: Sinus bradycardia: <50 bpm in the 2018 guideline (<60 bpm by convention). A sinus pause >=3 s is significant. Sinus tachycardia: >100 bpm. Inappropriate sinus tachycardia: resting sinus rate >100 bpm with a mean 24-h rate >90 bpm and no primary cause. Sinus arrhythmia: P-P variation >0.12 s or >10%, either respiratory or non-respiratory. () — 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS bradycardia guideline (Kusumoto FM et al., Circulation 2019;140:e382-e482); 2015 ACC/AHA/HRS SVT guideline (Page RL et al., Circulation 2016;133:e506-e574); 2019 ESC SVT guidelines (Brugada J et al.)
- Ectopic atrial rhythm / wandering atrial pacemaker / MAT — P axis and morphology: Ectopic atrial rhythm: P axis outside the sinus range, at <100 bpm (e.g. a low atrial focus gives a negative P in II, III and aVF with PR >=120 ms). Wandering atrial pacemaker: >=3 different P morphologies at <100 bpm. Multifocal atrial tachycardia: >=3 P morphologies with variable PP/PR at >100 bpm. (II, III, aVF, I, aVL, V1) — 2015 ACC/AHA/HRS SVT guideline (Page RL et al.); AHA/ACCF/HRS 2007 Part II statement list (Mason JW et al., Circulation 2007;115:1325-1332)
- Interatrial block — P duration and morphology: Partial interatrial block: P duration >=120 ms. Advanced interatrial block: P >=120 ms with a biphasic (+/-) P in II, III and aVF. Advanced block is linked to atrial fibrillation and stroke (Bayes syndrome). (II, III, aVF) — Bayes de Luna A et al., Interatrial blocks: a separate entity from left atrial enlargement - a consensus report, J Electrocardiol 2012;45:445-451
- QRS axis categories — RAD, right superior axis, fascicular blocks: Right axis deviation: +90 to +180 deg (moderate +90 to +120, marked +120 to +180). Right superior ('extreme') axis: -90 to +/-180 deg. Indeterminate axis: all limb leads isodiphasic. Left anterior fascicular block: axis -45 to -90 deg, qR in aVL, R-peak time in aVL >=45 ms, QRS <120 ms. Left posterior fascicular block: axis +90 to +180 deg in adults (after excluding other causes), rS in I/aVL, qR in III/aVF, QRS <120 ms. Children have age-specific normal ranges (neonates are normally right-axis). (I, II, III, aVF, aVL) — AHA/ACCF/HRS 2009 Part III (Surawicz B et al., Circulation 2009;119:e235-e240)
- QRS duration categories — adult and pediatric thresholds: Adults: QRS <110 ms normal; 110-119 ms = incomplete RBBB/LBBB or non-specific IVCD; >=120 ms = complete bundle branch block or wide QRS. Children have lower, age-specific thresholds: non-specific IVCD at >90 ms (8-16 y) and >80 ms (<8 y), with corresponding RBBB limits. () — AHA/ACCF/HRS 2009 Part III (Surawicz B et al., Circulation 2009;119:e235-e240)
- QT interval — measurement technique and rate correction: Measure from QRS onset to T end using the tangent method, in the lead with the longest QT. Do not include the U wave. In atrial fibrillation, average over about 10 beats. Prefer Fridericia or linear corrections (Framingham, Hodges) to Bazett at heart rates far from 60 bpm. With QRS >=120 ms, use JT or a QRS-adjusted QT. Prolonged: >=450 ms in men, >=460 ms in women. Short: <=390 ms. (II, V5 (longest-QT lead)) — AHA/ACCF/HRS 2009 Part IV (Rautaharju PM et al., Circulation 2009;119:e241-e250)
- Drug-induced QT prolongation — torsade risk thresholds: QTc >500 ms, or a rise of >60 ms from baseline, marks a high risk of torsade de pointes. Stop or review QT-prolonging drugs, correct potassium and magnesium, and monitor. () — AHA/ACCF Scientific Statement: Prevention of torsade de pointes in hospital settings (Drew BJ et al., Circulation 2010;121:1047-1060)
- Long and short QT syndromes — diagnostic QTc: LQTS: QTc >=480 ms on repeated 12-lead ECGs, or LQTS (Schwartz) score >3. Consider LQTS at QTc >=460 ms with unexplained syncope. Short QT syndrome: QTc <=360 ms plus a supporting feature (pathogenic variant, family history, or survived VT/VF); consider it at QTc <=320 ms. () — 2022 ESC Guidelines for ventricular arrhythmias and prevention of sudden cardiac death (Zeppenfeld K et al., Eur Heart J 2022;43:3997-4126)
- Normal ST elevation — upper limits at J point: V2-V3: <2 mm in men >=40 y, <2.5 mm in men <40 y, <1.5 mm in women. All other leads: <1 mm. Exceeding these in 2 contiguous leads (new) meets the STEMI ECG threshold. Lead-specific limits apply to V7-V9 and V3R-V4R. (V2-V3; all other leads) — Fourth Universal Definition of MI (Thygesen K et al., Eur Heart J 2019;40:237-269); 2023 ESC ACS guidelines (Byrne RA et al.)
- Normal versus pathological Q waves — duration and depth: Normal septal q waves in I, aVL, V5-V6 are narrow (<30-40 ms) and small. An isolated Q in III, and a Q in aVR, can be normal. Pathological Q: any Q >=20 ms (or QS) in V2-V3; or Q >=30 ms and >=1 mm deep (or QS) in 2 contiguous leads of the I/aVL/V6, V4-V6 or II/III/aVF groups; or R >=40 ms with R/S >=1 in V1-V2 (posterior equivalent). (I, aVL, V5-V6, III, aVR, V1-V3) — Fourth Universal Definition of MI (Thygesen K et al., Eur Heart J 2019;40:237-269)
- Early repolarization pattern — definition: J-point elevation (Jp) >=0.1 mV in >=2 contiguous inferior and/or lateral leads, with a notch or slur at the end of the QRS and QRS <120 ms. It is a common benign variant (young, male, athletes). 'Early repolarization syndrome' requires documented VF or polymorphic VT. (II, III, aVF, I, aVL, V4-V6) — Macfarlane PW et al., The early repolarization pattern: a consensus paper, JACC 2015;66:470-477; 2022 ESC VA/SCD guidelines
- Athlete ECG — normal training-related findings vs abnormal: Normal training-related findings: sinus bradycardia >=30 bpm, sinus arrhythmia, ectopic atrial or junctional escape rhythm, first-degree AV block (PR <400 ms), Mobitz I, incomplete RBBB, isolated QRS voltage criteria for LVH/RVH, early repolarization, the Black-athlete V1-V4 repolarization pattern, and juvenile T inversion V1-V3 under 16 y. Abnormal (needs evaluation): T inversion >=1 mm in >=2 contiguous leads outside aVR, III and V1 (except the variants above); ST depression; pathological Q; complete LBBB; QRS >=140 ms; QTc >=470 ms (men) or >=480 ms (women); PR >=400 ms; sinus rate <30 bpm or pauses >=3 s. () — International Recommendations for Electrocardiographic Interpretation in Athletes (Sharma S, Drezner JA et al., JACC 2017;69:1057-1075; Eur Heart J 2018;39:1466-1480)
- Pre-excitation (WPW pattern) — PR, delta wave, QRS: PR <120 ms, a delta wave (slurred QRS upstroke), QRS usually >=110-120 ms, and secondary ST-T changes. The delta-wave polarity helps localize the accessory pathway. (All 12; delta wave best in leads with a dominant R) — 2015 ACC/AHA/HRS SVT guideline (Page RL et al.); 2019 ESC SVT guidelines (Brugada J et al., Eur Heart J 2020;41:655-720)
- Electrode misplacement — limb-lead and precordial errors: LA/RA reversal: inverted P, QRS and T in I, and upright P/QRS in aVR, while the precordial leads look normal (this is how it differs from dextrocardia). Any reversal involving RL gives an almost flat line in one limb lead. V1-V2 placed too high (2nd-3rd intercostal space) gives a pseudo-incomplete RBBB pattern (rSr'), a negative P and poor R-wave progression. (I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF, V1-V2) — Batchvarov VN, Malik M, Camm AJ, Incorrect electrode cable connection during ECG recording, Europace 2007;9:1081-1090; AHA/ACCF/HRS 2007 Part I (Kligfield P et al.)
- Low QRS voltage — amplitude thresholds: Low voltage: all limb-lead QRS amplitudes <5 mm, and/or all precordial QRS amplitudes <10 mm. Causes include pericardial effusion, obesity, COPD, infiltrative cardiomyopathy and hypothyroidism. (I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF; V1-V6) — AHA/ACCF/HRS 2007 Part II statement list (Mason JW et al., Circulation 2007;115:1325-1332); Surawicz & Knilans, Chou's Electrocardiography in Clinical Practice, 6th ed.
- Pediatric ECG norms — age-dependent axis, R in V1, T in V1: Newborns normally have a rightward QRS axis (often +90 to +180 deg) and a dominant R in V1. The T in V1 becomes negative after about the first week; an upright T in V1 after that and before adolescence suggests RVH. Children also have shorter PR and QRS intervals and higher precordial voltages. Use age-specific percentile tables. (V1, V3R, limb leads) — ESC Task Force Guidelines for interpretation of the neonatal ECG (Schwartz PJ et al., Eur Heart J 2002;23:1329-1344); Rijnbeek PR et al., Eur Heart J 2001;22:702-711

**Missing in the book (add):**

- Sinus bradycardia — rate threshold: The guideline definition is a sinus rate <50 bpm, versus the traditional <60 bpm. Rates of 40-50 bpm at rest or during sleep are often physiologic, especially in the young and in athletes. Treatment depends on symptom correlation, not the number alone. (Rhythm strip (II)) — 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS Bradycardia Guideline, Table 3
- Sinus pause — duration: Sinus pause = the sinus node fires >3 s after the previous atrial depolarization. Sinus arrest = no evidence of sinus node depolarization. (Rhythm strip (II)) — 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS Bradycardia Guideline, Table 3
- Chronotropic incompetence — definition: The heart rate cannot rise to match demand. In practice this means failing to reach 80% of the expected heart-rate reserve on exercise testing; predicted maximum HR = 220 - age. Exercise ECG testing is Class IIa when symptoms are exercise-related. (Exercise ECG) — 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS Bradycardia Guideline, Table 3 and Figure 2
- Tachycardia-bradycardia syndrome — definition / management: Sinus bradycardia, ectopic atrial bradycardia or sinus pauses alternate with atrial tachycardia, flutter or AF. A pause of variable length often follows the end of the tachycardia (post-conversion pause). Pacing is Class I when symptomatic, both to relieve symptoms and to allow rate- or rhythm-control drugs. AF ablation is an alternative. (Rhythm strip; Holter) — 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS Bradycardia Guideline, Table 3; 2021 ESC Pacing Guidelines
- 2:1 AV block — localization clues: From 2:1 conduction alone the block cannot be classified as Mobitz I or II. Clues pointing to the AV node: narrow QRS, long PR in conducted beats, 3:2 Wenckebach periods elsewhere on the strip, improvement with atropine or exercise. Clues pointing to infranodal block: wide QRS or BBB, normal PR, worsening with exercise or atropine, improvement with carotid sinus massage. If the site is still unclear, use exercise testing (IIa) or an EP study (IIb). (Long rhythm strip (II, V1); full 12-lead for QRS width) — 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS Bradycardia Guideline, Table 3 and Figure 3; 2021 ESC Pacing Guidelines
- Conduction-ratio notation — convention: Ratios are written as P waves : QRS complexes (P : conducted beats). 3:2 = one blocked P in three. 3:1, 4:1 or longer runs of consecutive blocked P waves = high-grade (advanced) AV block, defined as >=2 consecutive non-conducted P waves at a constant physiologic rate with some AV conduction still present. (Rhythm strip) — 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS Bradycardia Guideline, Table 3
- Pseudo-AV block / mimics — exclusions before diagnosing second-degree block: Before diagnosing Mobitz I or II, confirm a constant P rate (<100 bpm). Exclude: (a) non-conducted premature atrial complexes (the early P' is often buried in the T); (b) vagal block, in which sinus slowing occurs together with PR prolongation or a dropped beat; (c) concealed junctional extrasystoles (advanced). (Rhythm strip; compare T-wave shapes for hidden P') — 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS Bradycardia Guideline, Table 3 (Mobitz definitions require a constant P rate; vagally mediated AV block); Barold SS, Hayes DL, Mayo Clin Proc 2001;76:44-57
- Vagally mediated AV block and nocturnal bradycardia — recognition / significance: Recognize vagal block by simultaneous sinus slowing and PR lengthening, mostly at night or at rest, and resolution with activity. Nocturnal bradycardia or AV block is not in itself an indication for pacing. Screen for sleep apnea. (Holter) — 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS Bradycardia Guideline, take-home message 2 and Table 3
- AV block / conduction disease — pacing regardless of symptoms: Acquired Mobitz II, high-grade or third-degree AV block without a reversible or physiologic cause: permanent pacing is Class I regardless of symptoms. Alternating bundle branch block: pacing is Class I. Syncope with bifascicular block and HV >=70 ms on EP study: pacing is Class I (ESC 2021). In other AV block, pace only when symptoms correlate. (12-lead (QRS/BBB pattern) + rhythm strip) — 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS Bradycardia Guideline, take-home message 5 and Figure 7; 2021 ESC Pacing Guidelines
- Athlete's ECG — bradyarrhythmia / AV-conduction thresholds: Normal training-related findings: sinus bradycardia >=30 bpm, sinus arrhythmia, ectopic atrial or junctional escape rhythm, first-degree AV block, and Mobitz I that disappears with exercise. Need evaluation: profound sinus bradycardia <30 bpm, PR >=400 ms (>=300 ms in children/adolescents per the 2025 EAPC statement), Mobitz II, high-grade block, third-degree block, and daytime Mobitz I that persists during exercise. (12-lead + rhythm strip) — Sharma S, Drezner JA et al., International Recommendations for ECG Interpretation in Athletes, JACC 2017;69:1057 / Eur Heart J 2018;39:1466; Finocchiaro G et al., EAPC consensus on abnormal ECG findings in athletes, Eur Heart J 2025
- Reversible causes of bradycardia/AV block — must exclude before labelling intrinsic disease: Drugs: beta-blockers, verapamil/diltiazem, digoxin, antiarrhythmics. Other causes: hyperkalaemia, hypothyroidism, acute MI or ischaemia, Lyme carditis, sleep apnea, hypothermia, raised intracranial pressure. Treat the cause first (Class I). (12-lead (e.g. peaked T in hyperkalaemia, scooped ST in digoxin effect)) — 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS Bradycardia Guideline, Tables 4, 7 and 9, Figures 2-3
- Unexplained AV block in younger adults — aetiology work-up: Second- or third-degree AV block without a clear cause in patients under about 60 should prompt a search for infiltrative or inflammatory disease, especially cardiac sarcoidosis, as well as Lyme, myocarditis, amyloid and laminopathy (LMNA). Advanced imaging (CMR, FDG-PET) is Class IIa. (12-lead) — 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS Bradycardia Guideline, Figure 3 and Table 9; 2021 ESC Pacing Guidelines; 2014 HRS Expert Consensus on Cardiac Sarcoidosis
- Post-TAVR conduction disturbances — ECG surveillance: New LBBB and high-degree AV block are common after TAVR. Compare pre- and post-procedure ECGs for new or worsening PR or QRS prolongation. Persistent complete or high-grade block needs pacing; new LBBB needs monitoring. (12-lead serial) — 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS Bradycardia Guideline, TAVR recommendations (take-home message 7); 2021 ESC Pacing Guidelines (pacing after TAVI)
- Complete AV block in atrial fibrillation — recognition: In AF, a slow and perfectly regular ventricular rhythm, with a junctional or ventricular escape, means complete AV block. Classic causes are digoxin toxicity and conduction disease. (Rhythm strip (II, V1)) — Surawicz & Knilans, Chou's Electrocardiography in Clinical Practice; 2021 ESC Pacing Guidelines (AF with bradycardia/AV block)
- Paroxysmal AV block — pattern: Sudden 1:1 conduction to complete block with a long ventricular asystole, often triggered by a PAC or PVC or by a slowing rate (phase-4 block). It suggests His-Purkinje disease. An idiopathic low-adenosine form also exists. It is a cause of syncope even when the resting ECG is near-normal (often with BBB). (Holter / loop recorder) — 2021 ESC Pacing Guidelines (paroxysmal AV block; adenosine-sensitive syncope); Aste M, Brignole M, Europace reviews on paroxysmal AV block
- Congenital complete AV block — association: Congenital complete AV block is linked to maternal anti-Ro/SSA antibodies (maternal SLE or Sjögren) and to congenital heart disease such as congenitally corrected TGA. The escape is usually a narrow junctional rhythm. (12-lead) — 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS Bradycardia Guideline, Table 9; 2021 ESC Pacing Guidelines (congenital AV block)
- AV dissociation in wide-QRS tachycardia — VT criterion: P waves dissociated from QRS complexes, or capture or fusion beats, in a wide-QRS tachycardia strongly favour VT. (II, V1, Lewis lead) — 2022 ESC Guidelines for ventricular arrhythmias and prevention of sudden cardiac death (Zeppenfeld et al.)

- WPW pattern vs WPW syndrome — definition: WPW pattern = ECG pre-excitation (short PR, delta, wide QRS) without symptoms or arrhythmia. WPW syndrome = pre-excitation plus symptomatic tachyarrhythmia (AVRT, pre-excited AF). The pattern is seen in about 0.15-0.25% of the population. Concealed APs conduct only retrogradely, give a normal resting ECG and are not linked to SCD. (12-lead in sinus rhythm) — 2019 ESC Guidelines for SVT (Eur Heart J 2020;41:655), WPW and concealed AP sections
- Ventricular pre-excitation (children) — age-specific thresholds: In children the PR is <90 ms and the QRS >90 ms. Adult cut-offs (120 ms) under-diagnose pre-excitation in children. (II for PR; widest QRS lead) — AHA/ACCF/HRS 2009 ECG Standardization Part III (Surawicz et al., Circulation 2009;119:e235)
- Pre-excitation as a mimic (pseudo-infarct, pseudo-LVH, pseudo-BBB) — interpretation rule: Negative delta waves create Q/QS complexes that mimic MI: an inferior 'pseudo-infarct' with inferoparaseptal APs, and a tall R in V1 with left APs mimicking posterior MI/RBBB. Secondary ST-T changes mimic ischaemia. Do not diagnose MI, LVH, BBB or fascicular block, and do not interpret the axis, on fully pre-excited beats. (II, III, aVF (pseudo-Q); V1-V2 (tall R); I, aVL) — Fourth Universal Definition of MI 2018 (Thygesen K et al., Eur Heart J 2019;40:237) - pre-excitation listed among conditions causing Q/QS waves without MI; AHA/ACCF/HRS 2009 Part III
- Variable degree of pre-excitation — recognition: The QRS is a fusion of AP and AV-node activation. Pre-excitation increases when AV-node conduction slows (vagal tone, adenosine, AV-nodal drugs, atrial pacing near the AP) and decreases with sympathetic tone. Left lateral APs often give subtle or minimal pre-excitation with a near-normal PR (look for a small q loss and slurring in I, aVL, V5-V6). The beat-to-beat 'concertina' effect is part of this. (I, aVL, V5-V6 (left lateral); V1) — 2019 ESC Guidelines for SVT (WPW section: pre-excitation may be intermittent or disappear in up to 35% over time); Eisenberger M et al., Europace 2010 (PR dispersion approach to confirm/exclude pre-excitation)
- Intermittent pre-excitation and exercise test — risk-stratification sign: Abrupt beat-to-beat loss of the delta wave, with the PR lengthening and the QRS normalising (on a resting ECG, Holter or exercise test), suggests an AP with a long refractory period, i.e. lower risk. This is imperfect: more than 20% still have AP ERP <250 ms. A gradual loss during exercise is NOT the same sign (it reflects enhanced AV-node conduction). (Rhythm strip, any lead with a clear delta) — PACES/HRS 2012 Expert Consensus on asymptomatic WPW pattern (Heart Rhythm 2012;9:1006); 2019 ESC Guidelines for SVT
- Asymptomatic pre-excitation — risk stratification and action: High-risk features: SPERRI <=250 ms, AP ERP <=250 ms, multiple APs, inducible AVRT. EP study (with isoprenaline) is recommended (Class I) for asymptomatic people in high-risk occupations and competitive athletes, and can be considered for others. Ablation is recommended when high-risk features are found. (Resting 12-lead; exercise ECG) — 2019 ESC Guidelines for SVT (asymptomatic pre-excitation recommendations); PACES/HRS 2012 Consensus
- Pre-excitation in athletes — screening classification: Ventricular pre-excitation (PR <120 ms + delta wave + QRS >=120 ms) is an ABNORMAL finding that needs further evaluation even in trained athletes. An isolated short PR without a delta wave in an asymptomatic athlete is not pre-excitation and needs no work-up. (12-lead screening ECG) — International Recommendations for ECG Interpretation in Athletes 2017 (Sharma S, Drezner JA et al., Eur Heart J 2018;39:1466 / JACC 2017;69:1057)
- Pre-excited AF — recognition-to-action safety rule: Seeing an irregular, broad, very fast tachycardia with varying QRS width means: do NOT give adenosine, verapamil/diltiazem, beta-blockers, digoxin or IV amiodarone (risk of VF). Use synchronized cardioversion if unstable; otherwise ibutilide or procainamide (flecainide/propafenone acceptable per ESC). Then ablate the AP. (Rhythm strip + V1, II) — 2019 ESC Guidelines for SVT (acute therapy of pre-excited AF); 2023 ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS AF Guideline (AF with pre-excitation section)
- Orthodromic AVRT vs typical AVNRT — ECG discriminators: AVRT: retrograde P clearly separate from the QRS (RP >70 ms; about 90 ms on the surface ECG), on the ST segment. Typical AVNRT: P hidden in or at the end of the QRS, seen as a pseudo-r' in V1 and pseudo-S in II/III/aVF (RP <70-90 ms). QRS alternans and ST depression are more frequent in AVRT but are not specific. Ipsilateral functional BBB that slows the tachycardia proves a free-wall AP (Coumel sign). (V1, II, III, aVF) — 2019 ESC Guidelines for SVT (narrow-QRS tachycardia differential, RP cut-offs, functional BBB in AVRT); 2015 ACC/AHA/HRS Adult SVT Guideline
- PJRT (permanent junctional reciprocating tachycardia) — criteria: Incessant long-RP narrow-QRS tachycardia (RP > PR) over a concealed, decremental, usually inferoparaseptal AP. Retrograde P waves are deeply negative in II, III and aVF, and the rate is often modest. It can cause tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy. Differential: atypical AVNRT, low atrial tachycardia. (II, III, aVF, V1) — 2019 ESC Guidelines for SVT (PJRT section)
- Fasciculoventricular pathway — criteria: Minimal, fixed pre-excitation with a short or normal PR and a slightly wide QRS. The degree of pre-excitation does not change with atrial pacing or adenosine. It is benign, does not support re-entry and needs no ablation, but it mimics superoparaseptal WPW. (V1-V3, I, aVF) — Sternick EB et al., Heart Rhythm 2005;2:1-6 (ECG of fasciculoventricular pathways vs anteroseptal/midseptal APs); 2019 ESC Guidelines for SVT (atypical pathways)

Pre-excitation with LVH or a short PR in hypertrophic or storage cardiomyopathy should prompt a search for PRKAG2, Danon or Pompe disease. (12-lead (pre-excitation + voltage/LVH)) — 2019 ESC Guidelines for SVT (Ebstein anomaly: 10-45% have right-sided APs, often multiple); 2023 ESC Guidelines for the management of cardiomyopathies (ECG red flags: short PR/pre-excitation in storage diseases)

- Narrow QRS tachycardia (systematic approach) — RP/PR algorithm: First decide regular or irregular. Irregular: AF, MAT, or flutter/AT with variable block. Regular: locate atrial activity. No visible P' or P' at the end of the QRS: typical AVNRT. Short RP (RP < PR) with P' in the ST segment: orthodromic AVRT (or AT with long PR). Long RP (RP > PR): AT, atypical AVNRT, PJRT, sinus tachycardia. More P' than QRS: AT or flutter (AV-node-independent). (II, III, aVF, V1 (look for P' in ST-T); long rhythm strip) — 2019 ESC SVT guidelines (Eur Heart J 2020;41:655), section 8.1 and Figure 'Differential diagnosis of narrow QRS tachycardia'; 2015 ACC/AHA/HRS SVT guideline, ECG differential diagnosis figure
- Orthodromic AVRT — ECG criteria: Regular narrow-QRS tachycardia, typically 150 to >220 bpm. Retrograde P' after the QRS, inside the ST-T segment, with a constant RP usually ≥90 ms and less than half the cycle length. Functional BBB on the same side as the pathway may slow the rate. ST depression and QRS alternans are common but nonspecific. Sinus-rhythm ECG may show pre-excitation (manifest pathway) or be normal (concealed pathway). (II, III, aVF, V1; compare with baseline sinus ECG) — 2019 ESC SVT guidelines, section 11.3 Atrioventricular re-entrant tachycardia (orthodromic AVRT description)
- Ventricular pre-excitation (WPW pattern) — ECG criteria: In adults: PR <120 ms, delta wave (slurred initial QRS), QRS ≥120 ms with secondary ST-T changes. WPW syndrome = this pattern plus tachyarrhythmia. Intermittent or abrupt loss of pre-excitation is a low-risk marker. (All 12 leads (delta-wave polarity localises pathway)) — AHA/ACCF/HRS Recommendations Part III: Intraventricular conduction disturbances (Surawicz B et al., Circulation 2009;119:e235-e240), ventricular pre-excitation; 2019 ESC SVT guidelines section 11.3 (risk stratification)
- Pre-excited atrial fibrillation — ECG recognition and risk: Fast, irregular, broad-QRS tachycardia with beat-to-beat changing QRS width/morphology, often >250 bpm. Shortest pre-excited RR interval ≤250 ms marks a high-risk pathway (risk of VF). AV-nodal blockers (adenosine, verapamil/diltiazem, beta-blockers, digoxin, amiodarone IV) are contraindicated. Transient AV block with persisting atrial activity unmasks AT or flutter. Gradual slowing and then re-acceleration suggests sinus tachycardia or automatic AT. Record a 12-lead ECG during adenosine. Use caution in pre-excitation (adenosine can precipitate fast AF). - Wide-QRS tachycardia — aVR algorithm (Vereckei): VT if in aVR: initial dominant R wave; or initial r or q >40 ms; or notch on the descending limb of a negative-onset QRS; or vi/vt <=1 (ventricular activation velocity in the initial 40 ms vs the terminal 40 ms) (aVR) — Vereckei A et al., Heart Rhythm 2008;5:89-98; ESC 2019 SVT guidelines (Brugada et al., Eur Heart J 2020;41:655), WCT criteria table

- Wide-QRS tachycardia — Mimics and default rule: Differential includes SVT with aberrancy/pre-existing BBB, pre-excited AF (fast, broad, irregular, varying QRS width, rates may exceed 250 bpm), antidromic AVRT, paced rhythm, hyperkalaemia or sodium-channel-blocker toxicity (very wide, sine-like), and motion/tremor artefact (narrow QRS marching through the artefact). - Polymorphic VT with normal QT — Distinction from TdP: Polymorphic VT without QT prolongation is usually ischaemic (acute coronary syndrome) or short-coupled idiopathic VF. - Bidirectional VT — Morphology and causes: Beat-to-beat alternation of the QRS axis/polarity in the frontal plane (often RBBB with alternating left/right axis). - Idiopathic fascicular VT — Morphology: RBBB-like with left superior axis (left posterior fascicle, most common) or right/inferior axis (left anterior fascicle). - Arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy — ECG criteria: Inverted T waves V1-V3 or beyond in individuals >14 y without complete RBBB (major). Epsilon wave (major in the 2010 Task Force criteria, minor in the 2020 Padua criteria). Terminal activation duration >=55 ms in V1-V3. VT with LBBB morphology and superior axis (major) or inferior axis (minor). >500 PVCs/24 h (V1-V3) — Marcus FI et al., 2010 Task Force Criteria, Circulation 2010;121:1533; Corrado D et al., 2020 Padua criteria, Int J Cardiol 2020;319:106; ESC 2022 VA/SCD guidelines
- Ventricular fibrillation — Fine VF vs asystole: Low-amplitude (fine) VF can look like asystole. Confirm 'flat line' in at least two leads with adequate gain and check lead connections before calling asystole (monitor leads) — European Resuscitation Council Guidelines 2021 (Adult Advanced Life Support); 2020 AHA Guidelines for CPR and ECC

**Missing in the book (add):**

- Hyperkalaemia — ECG progression: Rough sequence as K rises: tall, narrow-based, symmetric 'tented' T waves; then PR prolongation with flattening or loss of P waves; then QRS widening (bizarre morphology, possible pseudo-STEMI or Brugada-like pattern); then sine-wave pattern, bradycardia, high-grade AV block, VF or asystole. The ECG correlates poorly with serum K and can be near-normal even when K is dangerously high. Any ECG change in hyperkalaemia is an emergency. (T peaking clearest in V2–V4, II, III) — KDIGO Controversies Conference on potassium (Clase CM et al., Kidney Int 2020;97:42); UK Kidney Association Clinical Practice Guideline on Treatment of Acute Hyperkalaemia in Adults (2023); AHA 2020 ACLS guidelines, special circumstances (electrolyte-related arrest)
- Hypokalaemia — ECG signs: ST depression, flattening or inversion of the T wave, prominent U wave (often taller than the T in V2–V3), T–U fusion giving an apparent long QT (really QU). Raises the risk of TdP and digoxin toxicity; often coexists with hypomagnesaemia. (U waves best in V2–V3) — AHA/ACCF/HRS 2009 Part IV (Rautaharju et al., Circulation 2009;119:e241, U-wave section); Drew BJ et al., AHA/ACCF TdP prevention statement (Circulation 2010;121:1047)
- Calcium disorders — QT mechanism: Hypocalcaemia lengthens the QT by lengthening the ST segment; the T wave keeps a normal width. Hypercalcaemia shortens the QT through a short or absent ST segment (T wave seems to start at the end of the QRS); J (Osborn)-like waves occasionally appear in severe cases. (All leads; measure QT in the lead with the longest QT) — AHA/ACCF/HRS 2009 Part IV (Rautaharju et al., Circulation 2009;119:e241); Antzelevitch C et al., J-wave syndromes consensus (Heart Rhythm 2016;13:e295) for J waves of non-ischaemic cause
- QT interval — Measurement, correction and thresholds: Measure from the earliest QRS onset to the latest T end (tangent method), leaving out a separate U wave, in the lead with the longest QT (often II or V5). Bazett over-corrects at high heart rates and under-corrects at low ones; prefer Fridericia, Framingham or regression formulas. Prolonged: QTc ≥450 ms in men, ≥460 ms in women. Short: about ≤390 ms. High TdP risk: QTc >500 ms or a rise of >60 ms from baseline. Athlete referral thresholds: QTc ≥470 ms (men), ≥480 ms (women). With QRS ≥120 ms, use the JT interval or an adjusted QT. (II, V5 (longest-QT lead)) — AHA/ACCF/HRS 2009 Part IV (Rautaharju et al., Circulation 2009;119:e241); Drew BJ et al., Circulation 2010;121:1047; 2017 International criteria for ECG interpretation in athletes (Sharma et al., J Am Coll Cardiol 2017;69:1057)
- Congenital long QT syndrome — Diagnostic criteria and T morphology: Diagnose with QTc ≥480 ms on repeated 12-lead ECGs, or an LQTS (Schwartz) risk score >3. Consider it at QTc ≥460 ms with unexplained syncope. Typical T-wave patterns by genotype: LQT1 broad-based T; LQT2 low-amplitude, notched or bifid T; LQT3 long isoelectric ST with a late, peaked T. (Repeated 12-lead ECGs; lateral and precordial leads for T morphology) — ESC 2022 Guidelines for VA and prevention of SCD (Zeppenfeld et al., Eur Heart J 2022;43:3997); 2013 HRS/EHRA/APHRS Expert Consensus on inherited primary arrhythmia syndromes (Priori et al., Heart Rhythm 2013;10:1932)
- Short QT syndrome — Diagnostic threshold: Diagnose with QTc ≤360 ms plus one or more of: a pathogenic mutation, a family history of SQTS, or survival of VT/VF without structural heart disease. Consider it at QTc ≤320 ms. Tall, peaked, symmetric T waves with little or no ST segment. Exclude secondary causes first (hypercalcaemia, digoxin, hyperthermia). (Precordial leads) — ESC 2022 VA/SCD guidelines (Zeppenfeld et al., Eur Heart J 2022;43:3997)
- Brugada pattern / syndrome — Type 1 ECG: Type 1 (diagnostic) pattern: coved ST elevation ≥2 mm (0.2 mV) followed by a negative T wave in ≥1 right precordial lead (V1–V2) recorded in the 2nd, 3rd or 4th intercostal space, either spontaneous or induced by a sodium-channel blocker. Type 2 (saddleback) is not diagnostic. A drug-induced type 1 pattern alone is not enough for a diagnosis; it also needs arrhythmic syncope, documented VF/polymorphic VT or a family history. Fever, hyperkalaemia and sodium-channel-blocker toxicity can produce phenocopies. (V1–V2, including high intercostal spaces (2nd–3rd ICS)) — ESC 2022 VA/SCD guidelines (Zeppenfeld et al., Eur Heart J 2022;43:3997); Antzelevitch C et al., J-wave syndromes consensus / Shanghai score (Heart Rhythm 2016;13:e295); Bayés de Luna A et al., Current electrocardiographic criteria for diagnosis of Brugada pattern: a consensus report (J Electrocardiol 2012;45:433)
- Normal (male-pattern) ST elevation — Age/sex-specific upper limits: J-point elevation in V2–V3 is normal up to <2.5 mm in men <40 y, <2 mm in men ≥40 y and <1.5 mm in women; in other leads up to <1 mm. Concave elevation within these limits in a healthy young person is a normal variant. Above them, in the right clinical setting, it meets STEMI voltage criteria. The 2004 'early repolarisation' description belongs here. (V2–V3 thresholds; ≥2 contiguous leads) — AHA/ACCF/HRS 2009 Part VI: acute ischaemia/infarction (Wagner GS et al., Circulation 2009;119:e262); Fourth Universal Definition of MI (Thygesen K et al., Eur Heart J 2019;40:237); ESC 2023 ACS guidelines (Byrne RA et al., Eur Heart J 2023;44:3720)
- Early repolarisation syndrome — Diagnosis and high-risk ERP features: ERS = J-point elevation ≥1 mm in ≥2 contiguous inferior and/or lateral leads in a patient resuscitated from otherwise unexplained VF/polymorphic VT. In an asymptomatic person, ERP is a benign finding that needs no further testing. Higher-risk features: Jp ≥2 mm, inferior or global distribution, horizontal or descending ST segment, dynamic J waves. (II, III, aVF, I, aVL, V4–V6) — ESC 2022 VA/SCD guidelines (Zeppenfeld et al., Eur Heart J 2022;43:3997); Macfarlane PW et al., J Am Coll Cardiol 2015;66:470; Antzelevitch C et al., Heart Rhythm 2016;13:e295

- Cardiac amyloidosis — ECG red flags: Low QRS voltage (all limb leads <0.5 mV), or voltage disproportionately low for the LV wall thickness seen on echo (voltage–mass discordance). Pseudo-infarct pattern: Q/QS in V1–V3 without infarction. AV or intraventricular conduction disease; AF. A normal-voltage ECG does not exclude amyloidosis. (Limb leads (voltage); V1–V3 (pseudo-infarct)) — ESC Working Group position statement on diagnosis and treatment of cardiac amyloidosis (Garcia-Pavia P et al., Eur Heart J 2021;42:1554); ESC 2023 cardiomyopathy guidelines (Arbelo et al., Eur Heart J 2023;44:3503); 2023 ACC Expert Consensus Decision Pathway on cardiac amyloidosis (Kittleson MM et al., J Am Coll Cardiol 2023;81:1076)
- Arrhythmogenic (right ventricular) cardiomyopathy — Depolarisation/repolarisation criteria: T inversion in V1–V3 or beyond in people over 14 years without complete RBBB (major criterion). Epsilon wave: a small terminal deflection after the QRS in V1–V3 (major in the 2010 Task Force criteria, downgraded to minor in the 2020 Padua criteria). Terminal activation duration ≥55 ms in V1–V3. Low limb-lead voltage. PVCs or VT with LBBB morphology (superior axis suggests RV origin). Padua 2020 adds left-ventricular criteria: inferolateral T inversion and low voltage. (V1–V3 (also inferolateral for left-dominant forms)) — Marcus FI et al., 2010 Task Force Criteria (Circulation 2010;121:1533); Corrado D et al., 2020 Padua criteria (Int J Cardiol 2020;319:106); ESC 2023 cardiomyopathy guidelines (Arbelo et al., Eur Heart J 2023;44:3503)
- Acute pericarditis / tamponade — ECG findings: Pericarditis: new, widespread concave ST elevation without reciprocal depression (except in aVR and often V1), plus PR depression with PR elevation in aVR. Stages evolve over days to weeks (ST normalises before T inversion, unlike infarction). New widespread STE or PR depression is one of the clinical diagnostic criteria (with pain, rub and effusion). Large effusion or tamponade: low QRS voltage, electrical alternans, sinus tachycardia. (Diffuse limb and precordial leads; aVR for reciprocal PR elevation/ST depression) — 2015 ESC Guidelines for pericardial diseases (Adler Y et al., Eur Heart J 2015;36:2921); 2025 ESC Guidelines for the management of myocarditis and pericarditis (Eur Heart J 2025;46:3952)
- Sodium-channel blocker toxicity (e.g. tricyclic overdose) — ECG markers: Sinus tachycardia; QRS ≥100 ms (seizure risk) and ≥160 ms (ventricular arrhythmia risk); terminal R in aVR ≥3 mm or R/S in aVR ≥0.7; rightward terminal 40-ms QRS axis; QT prolongation; Brugada-like pattern. QRS widening is treated with hypertonic sodium bicarbonate. (aVR, I, limb leads for QRS duration) — AHA 2023 Focused Update on management of patients with cardiac arrest or life-threatening toxicity due to poisoning (Lavonas EJ et al., Circulation 2023;148:e149); Boehnert MT, Lovejoy FH, N Engl J Med 1985;313:474; Liebelt EL et al., Ann Emerg Med 1995;26:195
- Digoxin toxicity — Arrhythmias: Toxicity combines increased automaticity with depressed conduction. Arrhythmias: frequent PVCs or ventricular bigeminy, atrial tachycardia with AV block, accelerated junctional rhythm, 'regularised' AF (AF with complete AV block and a junctional escape), bidirectional VT, high-grade AV block or severe sinus bradycardia. Worsened by hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hypercalcaemia and renal failure. Treat life-threatening toxicity with digoxin-specific Fab. 'Digoxin effect' (scooped ST) does not indicate toxicity. (Rhythm strip (II, V1)) — AHA 2023 Focused Update on poisoning-related cardiac arrest/toxicity (Lavonas EJ et al., Circulation 2023;148:e149)
- Hypothermia — ECG findings: Osborn (J) waves: a positive hump at the J point, larger at lower temperatures. Sinus bradycardia; prolonged PR, QRS and QT; AF with slow ventricular response; shivering artefact. Risk of VF and asystole rises sharply below about 28–30 °C. The J waves resolve with rewarming. (Inferior and lateral leads (V3–V6, II)) — Antzelevitch C et al., J-wave syndromes expert consensus (Heart Rhythm 2016;13:e295); Wilderness Medical Society Clinical Practice Guidelines for Out-of-Hospital Evaluation and Treatment of Accidental Hypothermia: 2019 Update (Dow J et al., Wilderness Environ Med 2019;30:S47)
- Takotsubo syndrome — ECG pattern: Acute phase: ST elevation, often anterior and extending beyond one coronary territory, with less reciprocal depression; sometimes ST depression only. Later: deep, widespread T inversion with QTc prolongation over days, then gradual resolution. The ECG cannot reliably tell takotsubo from STEMI, so coronary angiography is needed. (Precordial V2–V5, limb leads) — International Expert Consensus Document on Takotsubo Syndrome, Part I (Ghadri JR et al., Eur Heart J 2018;39:2032); ESC 2023 ACS guidelines (Byrne RA et al., Eur Heart J 2023;44:3720), MINOCA differential
- Pulmonary embolism — Most frequent finding and RV-strain markers: Sinus tachycardia is the most common abnormality and may be the only one in milder PE. These signs are associated with RV dysfunction and worse outcome. The ECG neither confirms nor excludes PE; diagnosis relies on clinical probability, D-dimer and CTPA. **Missing in the book (add):**

- Pacemaker code — NBG (NASPE/BPEG) generic code: Position I = chamber paced, II = chamber sensed (A/V/D/O), III = response to sensing (I inhibit, T trigger, D dual, O none), IV = rate modulation (R), V = multisite pacing. Learners must map VVI, AAI, DDD, VDD, DDDR, VOO/DOO (magnet) to expected ECG behaviour. (Rhythm strip (II, V1)) — Bernstein AD et al. Revised NASPE/BPEG generic code, PACE 2002;25:260-4
- Pacing stimulus — Detection and appearance on the ECG: Bipolar pacing spikes are often tiny or invisible on the surface ECG; unipolar spikes are large. Digital electrocardiographs need high-rate pacemaker-pulse detection and may display marker spikes, or miss or falsely insert them, so a 'spike' on a filtered tracing may be an annotation. Always look at several leads for a spike before an atrial or ventricular complex. (All 12 leads; rhythm strip) — Kligfield P et al. AHA/ACCF/HRS Recommendations for the Standardization and Interpretation of the ECG, Part I, Circulation 2007;115:1306-24 (pacemaker pulse detection); IEC 60601-2-25
- Pacemaker malfunction — Failure to capture: A pacing stimulus delivered outside the tissue's refractory period that is not followed by a P wave (atrial) or QRS (ventricular). Causes: threshold rise, lead dislodgement or fracture, hyperkalaemia, antiarrhythmic drugs, MI or fibrosis, battery depletion. Do not call a spike falling in the refractory period (e.g., just after an intrinsic QRS during undersensing) a capture failure. (Rhythm strip (II, V1)) — Mason JW et al. AHA/ACCF/HRS Part II statement list, Circulation 2007;115:1325-32 ('failure to capture, atrial/ventricular'); Ellenbogen KA et al. Clinical Cardiac Pacing, Defibrillation and Resynchronization Therapy, 5th ed. 2017
- Pacemaker malfunction — Undersensing (failure to inhibit): Pacing spikes appear inappropriately after intrinsic P waves or QRS complexes, i.e., the device behaves as if it did not see them; the intervals do not reset. Differentiate from magnet/asynchronous mode, functional undersensing in blanking/refractory periods (e.g., PVC in PVARP), and fusion/pseudofusion. (Rhythm strip) — Mason JW et al. AHA/ACCF/HRS Part II statement list, Circulation 2007;115:1325-32 ('failure to inhibit'); Ellenbogen KA et al. 5th ed. 2017
- Pacemaker malfunction — Oversensing / failure to pace (output): Pauses longer than the lower-rate interval without spikes and without intrinsic activity, because the device sensed non-cardiac or far-field signals (myopotentials, EMI, T-wave, far-field R, crosstalk, lead-fracture noise). Rule out normal features before calling oversensing: rate hysteresis, sleep/rest rate, and RV-pacing-avoidance algorithms. (Rhythm strip) — Mason JW et al. Part II statement list, Circulation 2007 ('failure to pace'); Glikson M et al. 2021 ESC pacing guidelines; Ellenbogen KA et al. 5th ed. 2017
- Paced rhythm — Fusion and pseudofusion beats: Fusion beat: a QRS shaped by both the paced and the intrinsic wavefronts, intermediate in morphology. Pseudofusion beat: the spike falls on an intrinsic QRS that it does not change. Both are normal timing coincidences when the intrinsic rate is close to the pacing rate. They are not malfunctions. (Any; best seen in V1, II) — Ellenbogen KA et al. Clinical Cardiac Pacing, Defibrillation and Resynchronization Therapy, 5th ed. 2017; Barold SS, Stroobandt RX, Sinnaeve AF. Cardiac Pacemakers and Resynchronization Step-by-Step
- DDD pacing — Upper-rate behaviour and pacemaker-mediated tachycardia (PMT): Atrial rates above the upper tracking rate cause pacemaker Wenckebach (progressive lengthening of the AS-VP interval with a dropped beat) or fixed 2:1 block when the P-P interval is shorter than the total atrial refractory period. PMT (endless-loop tachycardia) is a regular ventricular-paced tachycardia at or near the upper tracking rate, sustained by retrograde P waves (often triggered by a PVC or loss of atrial capture). A magnet terminates it. (Rhythm strip; look for retrograde P in II, III, aVF) — Glikson M et al. 2021 ESC pacing guidelines; Ellenbogen KA et al. 5th ed. 2017
- Biventricular pacing (CRT) — Paced QRS morphology: Effective BiV pacing typically gives a narrower paced QRS than baseline, a dominant R in V1 (RBBB-like), and a negative or QS QRS in lead I (right axis). Reversion to an LBBB-like pattern (negative V1, positive I, wider QRS) suggests loss of LV capture. Loss of RV capture gives a wide RBBB-like QRS with a QS in I. CRT candidacy: LBBB with QRS >=150 ms is Class I (130-149 ms IIa). (I, V1 (key), all 12 leads) — Barold SS, Herweg B. Usefulness of the 12-lead ECG in the follow-up of patients with cardiac resynchronization devices, Parts I-II, Cardiol J 2011;18:476-86 and 610-24; Glikson M et al. 2021 ESC Guidelines on cardiac pacing and CRT
- Conduction system pacing (His bundle / left bundle branch area) — ECG capture criteria: His pacing: selective capture = isoelectric interval after the spike with a native-like QRS; non-selective capture = pseudo-delta wave fused to the spike with a narrow QRS. LBBA pacing: paced QRS with RBBB-like V1 (qR/rSR'). LBB capture is supported by a stimulus-to-V6 R-wave peak time <75 ms (narrow baseline QRS or isolated RBBB) or <80 ms (LBBB/IVCD), a V6-V1 interpeak interval >44 ms, or a sudden V6 RWPT change during threshold testing. (V1, V6, I, all 12 leads) — Burri H et al. EHRA clinical consensus statement on conduction system pacing implantation, Europace 2023;25:1208-36 (endorsed by APHRS, CHRS, LAHRS); Chung MK et al. 2023 HRS/APHRS/LAHRS guideline on cardiac physiologic pacing, Heart Rhythm 2023
- Acute MI in ventricular paced rhythm — Ischaemia criteria: RV pacing hides acute MI in the same way as LBBB. Apply Sgarbossa-type rules: concordant ST elevation >=1 mm in leads with positive QRS; concordant ST depression >=1 mm in V1-V3 (V4-V6 can be included in paced rhythm); excessively discordant ST elevation, best judged as a proportion (Smith-modified ST/S ratio <=-0.25) rather than the original >=5 mm. In paced rhythm the modified criteria had about 81% sensitivity and 96% specificity for occlusion MI. If symptoms persist, the patient goes to a primary PCI strategy. (All 12 leads; concordant ST depression V1-V6) — Thygesen K et al. Fourth Universal Definition of MI 2018, Circulation 2018;138:e618-51; Byrne RA et al. 2023 ESC Guidelines for ACS, Eur Heart J 2023;44:3720-826 (atypical ECG presentations incl. paced rhythm); Dodd KW et al. Ann Emerg Med 2021;78:517-29; Kontos MC et al. 2022 ACC Expert Consensus Decision Pathway on chest pain in the ED, JACC 2022
- Post-pacing T waves — Cardiac memory: After a period of ventricular pacing (or intermittent LBBB/WPW), intrinsic beats can show deep T-wave inversion following the vector of the paced QRS. This mimics anterior ischaemia. Favouring memory over LAD ischaemia: positive T in aVL, positive or isoelectric T in I, and maximal precordial T inversion greater than the T inversion in III (all three criteria together: high sensitivity and specificity). (I, aVL, III, V1-V6) — Shvilkin A et al. Vectorcardiographic and electrocardiographic criteria to distinguish new and old LBBB/cardiac memory from ischemia, Heart Rhythm 2005;2:1072-8 (cardiac memory criteria); Rosenbaum MB et al. Am J Cardiol 1982;50:213-22
- Paced QRS — Metabolic / drug effects (hyperkalaemia): Hyperkalaemia (typically K >7 mEq/L) and class IC drugs widen the paced QRS and increase stimulus-to-QRS latency, raise thresholds, and can cause Wenckebach-type or 2:1 exit block up to complete loss of capture. The atrium is affected before the ventricle. An unusually wide paced QRS (>200 ms) should prompt checking potassium and drugs. (Rhythm strip; all leads for QRS width) — Barold SS, Herweg B. The effect of hyperkalaemia on cardiac rhythm devices, Europace 2014;16:467-76
- Ventricular pacing (VVI) — Retrograde VA conduction / pacemaker syndrome: With VVI pacing, look for retrograde P waves in the ST segment or T wave after each paced QRS (inverted in II, III, aVF). VA conduction causes pacemaker syndrome and can trigger PMT in tracking modes. This is a reason to prefer AV-synchronous pacing in patients in sinus rhythm. (II, III, aVF) — Glikson M et al. 2021 ESC Guidelines on cardiac pacing and CRT (mode selection, pacemaker syndrome)
- DDD pacing — Ventricular safety pacing and RV-pacing-minimisation algorithms: Ventricular safety pacing: a signal sensed early after an atrial pace triggers a ventricular stimulus at a short AV delay (about 100-120 ms), often falling on an intrinsic QRS. MVP-type and AV-search algorithms allow long PR intervals or a single non-conducted P wave without a ventricular stimulus. All of these are normal behaviour, not malfunction. (Rhythm strip) — Glikson M et al. 2021 ESC pacing guidelines (algorithms to minimise RV pacing); Chung MK et al. 2023 HRS cardiac physiologic pacing guideline, Heart Rhythm 2023
- Leadless pacemaker — ECG appearance and magnet response: Leadless RV pacemakers (e.g., Micra, Aveir) give VVI(R) or VDD pacing from the RV septum/apex region with LBBB-like morphology and small spikes. The Micra has no magnet response, so a magnet test cannot be relied on. (All 12 leads) — Glikson M et al. 2021 ESC Guidelines on cardiac pacing and CRT (leadless pacing section); manufacturer manuals
